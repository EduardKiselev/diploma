Drone-aided Detection of Weeds: Transfer Learning
for Embedded Image Processing
Iaroslav Koshelev, Maxim Savinov, Alexander Menshchikov, Andrey Somov
Abstract —In this article, we address the problem of Hogweed
detection using a drone equipped with an RGB and multispectral
cameras. We study two approaches (i) offline detection running
on the orthophoto of the area scanned within the mission, and (ii)
realtime scanning from the frames stream directly on the edge
device performing the flight mission. We show that by fusing the
information from an additional multispectral camera installed
on the drone, there is an opportunity to boost the detection
quality which then can be preserved even with a single RGB
camera setup by introduction of additional Convolution Neural
Network (CNN) trained with transfer learning to produce the
fake multispectral images directly from the RGB stream. We
show that this approach helps either eliminate the multispectral
hardware from the drone or if only the RGB camera is at
hand, to boost the segmentation performance by the cost of
slight increase in computational budget. To support this claim we
have performed an extensive study of networks performance in
simulations of both realtime and offline modes, where we achieve
at least 1.1% increase in terms of mean intersection over union
(mIoU) metric when evaluated on RGB stream from the camera
and 1.4% when evaluated on ortophoto data. Our results show
that the proper optimization guarantees a complete elimination
of the multispectral camera from the flight mission by adding a
preprocessing stage to segmentation network without the loss of
quality.
Index Terms —Semantic segmentation, deep learning, transfer
learning, edge computing
I. I NTRODUCTION
CONTINUOUSLY growing human population imposes
strict demands to agricultural industry in terms of im-
proving the crop yields and making the food production
efficient. At the same time, high efficiency can be achieved
by controlling and removing the weeds. To overcome this
challenge a high number of the state-of-the-art technologies
have been applied in the scope of precision agriculture [1].
Distribution of weeds and, in particular, Hogweed of Sos-
nowsky, is a quickly growing problem for agricultural industry.
Hogweed of Sosnowsky is a weed that has spread across
Eurasia region, including European and Asian countries, Great
Britain and Russia, as well as local parts of North American
continent. Hogweed typically grows for up to 5 mand may
have the diameter for up to 12 cm. It is capable of producing
thousands of seeds which are then distributed by wind across
large area. The plant produces toxins which are hazardous for
human, but at the same time hogweed creates a danger to other
crops including the farming ones by affecting their ecosystem.
This hazardous weed must, therefore, be precisely identified
and removed.

localization: (i) inspection by human, (ii) satellite imaging, and

In this work, we rely on the UA V option due to its practical
feasibility. The most commonly utilized sensor setup for
this platform consists of a single video-camera, capable of
producing high-quality RGB images and a Global Navigation
Satellite System sensor providing geographic coordinates for
the measurements from the camera. Additional multispectral
camera is also typically introduced for the crops monitoring
missions to allow for a computation of vegetation indices.
The same sensor could also be utilized to increase the crops
detection accuracy as it was shown for example in [2], but
exploiting two video cameras on board is not weight and cost
wise.
In this paper we investigate the trade offs between seman-
tic segmentation quality of crops and the imaging sensors
setup complexity. We use a task of hogweed detection as a
playground and propose a solution based on the advances of
deep learning, which allows for the complete avoidance of a
multispectral camera from the mission without any detoriation
of segmentation performance compared to the multiple cam-
eras platform. More specifically, we report on the following
novelty:
1) Dataset collection, alignment and labeling. We propose
to label the hogweed plants by transforming a sequence
of overlapping frames into a single orthophoto. It en-
ables labeling the crops in the area of interest only once
without repetition in intersecting parts of the images.
The corresponding labels are then back-transformed to
the camera frames using a greedy features-based image
alignment procedure. Similar procedure is applied to
align the RGB and multispectral data, with the cor-
responding orthophotos acting as a reference. As a
result our approach the labeled and aligned RGB and
multispectral images are produced fast without a need
to synchronize frame streams coming from two separate
camera sensors. To the best of our knowledge, such
methodology was never reported before.
2) Removal of multispectral camera from the setup by
replacement with the preprocessing network. Instead of
using two cameras setup, we propose to adopt a transfer
learning procedure and learn a convolution neural net-
work from the collected paired data to mimic multispec-
tral images. Such images are then synthesised during
preprocessing stage, which allows to completely avoid
multispectral hardware in the aerial platform without any

II. R ELATED WORK
A. Weed Detection
Traditional approaches of weeds localization include manual
inspection with special handheld equipment, which is done
either by foot or with utilization of transport machines like
cars or tractors [3]. Although these methods can still be in
favour for farmers, there is a certain shift towards the systems
allowing for monitoring of larger areas while preventing the
spread of seeds by feet and wheels. That is why the modern
development of methods involving the Unmanned Aerial Ve-
hicles (UA V) and even satellites imaging starts to gain close
attention for the purposes of more effective human-free ways
of crops monitoring and weed control [4].
Currently satellite imaging provides accurate images across
huge areas with different spectral bands lying far beyond just
conventional visual spectrum, provided by ordinary RGB cam-
era. However, satellite imaging still does not allow to perform
precise crops localization due to the large Ground Sampling
Distance (GSD), since resolution of the best available satellite
images does not exceed 30 cm/px . Typical size of the hogweed
leafs is around the same 30 cm, which results in a situation,
when a single hogweed plant is resolved into image area of
several pixels, which makes its detection nearly impossible.
Also satellite monitoring system is vulnerable to the weather
changes, since it becomes impossible to capture plants at a
cloudy weather. At the same time, UA Vs enable to select
GSD from millimeters to meters per pixel depending on the
needs by varying altitude during the mission. As for UA Vs it
is still impossible to perform monitoring in rainy conditions,
the applicability of them is far more flexible, than satellites.
Currently, the limitation on equipment and computational
resources the UA Vs can carry, as well as the area UA Vs can
cover per a single mission, are strongly bounded by their lifting
capacity and power supply resources. This issue poses the
problem of effective management of sensors and computer
vision algorithms used for monitoring.
Nowadays in precision agriculture a multisensor setup is
typically used. It combines a traditional RGB sensor together
with the infrared one. For the latter the Near Infrared band
(NIR) is usually considered. Compared to a single RGB
camera, the multispectral cameras enable crops monitoring
with much greater precision as well as direct calculation of
their vegetation characteristics. This is done at a cost of
additional hardware carried on board during the mission, and
additional efforts for accurate sensors synchronization and
images alignment.
B. Image Analysis in Precision Agriculture
Even before the raise of deep learning, many algorithms
were proposed to perform a high-level vision of the images.Most of them rely on handcrafted distinct features of the
object of interest, and it was shown that this principle is
suitable for the agriculture as well [5]. Now almost all state-
of-the-art approaches involve the usage of CNNs and even
transformers. Ronneberger et al. [6] were one of the pioneers
who introduced a U-shaped neural architecture for the task
of image segmentation, which is still applied in a real high-
level vision tasks. This architecture was shown to produce
state-of-the-art results in agricultural tasks as well and being
capable to run on the edge device. For example, in [7] U-
shaped architecture was deployed to the unmanned ground
vehicle (UGV) in order to perform a robust estimate a pixel-
wise labeling of the images into crop and weed. It was shown,
that this setup outperforms previous state-of-the-art approaches
and improves the accuracy of crop-weed classification without
requiring a retraining of the model. In [8] the authors reported
on a comparison between other segmentation CNNs which
showed that U-Net performance is slightly lower than the com-
peting SegNet and RefineNet with ResNet backbone. However,
the computational complexity of the latter two models does not
allow for deploying them to an embedded unit and perform
the inference in real-time during the mission.
In our previous work [8] we focused on the optimization of
the most relevant FCNN architectures for inference on board
the Single Board Computer (SBC). The point of this work
was the feasibility of the application of such a drone with AI
on board in Precision Agriculture (PA). However, the dataset
was collected during a single day. It does not contain different
phases of the hogweed growth. Thus, the capabilities of the
FCNN, trained on such a dataset are limited. Furthermore, the
data were collected using the RGB camera only. The current
paper proposes an approach, that enhances the capabilities of
the monitoring system in several ways.
First, the dataset used in this study was collected in the same
area with a weekly period of 2.5 months. It contains the data on
the growth phases of the hogweed during this timespan. Hence,
such a dataset is valuable for both AI and PA areas. Moreover,
the dataset was collected using both RGB and multispectral
cameras. Additional channels give more information about the
object of interest and improve the accuracy and robustness
of the neural network. The current research’s contribution is
the enhancement of the neural network capabilities due to the
generation of artificial NIR channels. It provides a significant
advantage in terms of both accuracy of predictions and fea-
sibility such a monitoring system in practice. Application of
the proposed approach allows rid of the multispectral camera
for hogweed detection. Thus it makes the monitoring system
cheaper, more sustainable, and prolongs the flight time.
It should be noted that deployment of neural architecture
to the edge device is not a trivial task [9] and various opti-
mization techniques were proposed to overcome this problem.
A hardware-based optimization for binary weight CNNs was
proposed in [10] where the arithmetic core was optimized
by elimination of the need for expensive multiplications, as
well as by reduction of I/O bandwidth and storage. Typically,
however, most well known techniques to accelerate inference
are software-based, and include architecture-related optimiza-
tions which are performed either manually or automatically via

C. Intelligent Monitoring Systems in Precision Agriculture
There are different types of autonomous vehicles, which al-
low a precise monitoring of crops. Typically they are classified
into two groups, UA Vs, that operate in the air, and UGVs,
that operate on the ground. As UGVs are able to perform
individual plant phenotyping, characteristics monitoring and
even manipulations with crops and weeds [12], in crops
monitoring tasks the priority is given to UA Vs due to their
ability to cover large areas relatively fast without disseminating
the seeds.
Typically, the UA Vs are equipped with an RGB camera
capturing the crops underneath in a visual spectrum, and the
machine vision algorithms are processing the images from
this camera in order to get the desired output. Typically such
processing is done offline after the mission is complete [13]
since the frames stream is stored on an internal storage of
UA V [14] or is transferred to the intermediate server during
the mission by a wireless protocol [15]. Since both cases are
not practically feasible as they require additional efforts and do
not allow for fast decision making, it is important to consider
inference being performed directly on the edge device [16].
This is especially vital for the hogweed detection, as the weed
can spread very fast by generating up to 100,000 seeds a year
which are quickly disseminated by wind. A proper detection
and elimination of each poisonous plant is essential, and the
edge computing enables it by providing the instant information
on geolocation of the plant.
In order to improve the monitoring outcomes, additional
information can be provided to the vision algorithm in a form
of a separate multispectral frame stream. Typically this is
done to allow direct calculations of vegetation indices such as
normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) or normalized
difference red edge index (NDRE) [17]. For example, in [18]
authors report a UA V-based setup consisting of RGB and
NIR-retrofitted camera, which performs imaging missions over
rice and corn fields in the Philippines. They comment on
using dual camera setup in order to compute precise NDVI
of the crops. A survey performed by Radoglou-Grammatikis
et al . [19] extends the list of possible multispectral camera
usages towards optimization of the image acquisition system
and water stress indices. At the same time robust quantitative

sampling visually plausible near infrared spectral band from
the satellite and aerial RGB observations respectively.
III. M ETHODS
A. Data Collection
Due to the individual features of a hogweed plant, large size
of its leaves and large height, we found it possible to perform
an aerial collection of the data. We have performed two flight
missions in two separate days with the time difference of
two weeks in order to cover crops in different growth stages,
scanning the two neighboring fields in xcity, ycountry,2and
we used images captured from both fields separately for train
and evaluation purposes. In both missions we collected RGB
images of size 3000×4000 (height ×width) pixels using
DJI-FC330 camera mounted on a DJI drone capturing under-
neath field in spatial locations predefined during the mission
planning. Specifically for the flight collecting training data,
we have mounted an additional MAPIR Survey3 multispectral
camera, capturing red (660 nm), green (550 nm) and NIR (850
nm) channels of the same spatial resolution at a predefined
frequency of 0.5 Frames Per Second (FPS) and we denote the
data from this camera as RGN.
In this research, we generate a fake infrared channel using
a Generative Adversarial Neural Network. This approach is
promising from a practical point of view. There are several
alignment issues that we address using an artificial channel.
1) First of all, the camera DJI-FC330 is attached to the drone’s
gimbal. It allows a precise control of camera position during
the flight according to the mission requirements. The gimbal
is also used to stabilize the camera in case of wind gusts.
On the other hand the multispectral camera is fixed and is
always aligned with the drone’s pose. Hence there is no
way to compensate for its shift in case of any disturbances,
and the resulted images from two cameras are not aligned
with each other. 2) Due to different design of the lenses,
the apertures of cameras are 20 centimeters away from each
other, so both the field of view and the sensors illumination
varies between the two. This effect also contributes to the

B. Data Processing and Labeling
The collected dataset consists of two independent sequences
of frames, which are not synchronized between each other.
In order to align them, we propose to fuse both frames
streams into a corresponding orthophotos, and we use an
OpenDroneMap software for this3. Once the step is com-
pleted, it is convenient to label all the regions of interest
within the RGB orthophoto, using any raster image processing
software.
In order to align the images and construct a paired dataset
for training, we have applied a greedy region-based procedure.
At the first step, the two orthophotos are globally aligned based
on the best affine transformation between corresponding ORB
features [26]. At the second stage we divide both orthophotos
and the labels into overlapping patches of size 256×256px.
We use the same alignment procedure applied to each patch

Constructing training data for the online scanning regime
involves additional RGB frames stream used to create the
orthophoto. For this scenario, an intermediate stage based
on the same alignment procedure is required in order to
find the position of the entire frame in the RGB orthophoto.
The selected region is cropped from both orthophotos and
labels. Due to the nature of texturing step used to synthesise
orthophotos, the best match between original frame and its
region inside the orthophoto is achieved within the central
part of the frame. Thus we run the patch based refinement
only on the central part of size 2000×2000 pixels. We have
performed this procedure in order to sample both train and test
data with random crops of size 128×128for training purposes
and256×256 for the test. Since for the test dataset we do
not collect multispectral images, we exclude this data from
the discussed sampling procedure. Overall we have collected
more than 20000 crops for training purposes and 83 crops for
evaluation, and we carefully checked the correctness of the
resulted labels by additional look at the images in the latter.
We should note, that the region, where performed data
collection contained both vegetating (young) hogweed crops
and those, which have already faded (old). Since the pro-
cedures required to eliminate these two types of hogweed
plants can vary, we have labeled such cases with different
semantic classed to learn the segmentation network to dis-
tinguish between them, and facilitating an effective practical
application of our work. For all our reported data we have
used a manual labelling procedure in GIMP graphic editor4. It
should be noted that only RGB orthophotos are required for
labelling, since the produced labels can be back-projected to
the corresponding RGN orthophotos and original frames using
procedure discussed above. We release the dataset consisting
of both training, validation and testing samples, where we
included both labeled orthophoto together with a paired crops

C. Neural Network Architectures
In this study, we do not provide extensive experiments
with neural network architectures, since our main goal is to
show the approaches to improve the performance of exist-
ing predefined architecture when additional data is at hand.
However, we do compare different architectures in two modes,
namely computationally more and less intensive, and we do the
special emphasis on the latter one in this work due to practical
concerns. We depart from the high capacity neural networks
and use a SegNet architecture [27] with 29.5M parameters
for segmentation part and an encoder-decoder network with
residual blocks [28] and 7.8M parameters for the fake NIR
synthesis. Both architectures were proven to perform well with
the general-purpose segmentation and synthesis tasks respec-
tively [29] [30]. Targeting a balance between the performance
and inference speed when evaluated on an embedded system,
we have selected well-known U-Net architectures for both
segmentation and synthesis tasks in the less computationally
intensive regime, since its superiority for satisfying both goals
was shown in recent papers [7]. It is a fully Convolution Neural
Network (CNN) which was initially developed specifically for
semantic segmentation task, however it was also shown, that
this network can efficiently solve image to image tasks [31].
Being an encoder-decoder architecture, the main benefit is that
it allows processing and analysis of images at different scales,
which allows to greatly increase its receptive field without
the increase of computational complexity. Targeting the fast
inference speeds, for segmentation network we have used a
U-Net backbone consisting of 3 blocks in both encoder and
decoder parts with 17.3M parameters total, and a backbone
with 2 blocks and 2.2M parameters for synthesis. We found
the capacity of these networks good enough to both obtain
a good quality in semantic segmentation task, and run them
with high speed on an embedded system.
D. Training and Implementation Details
We have conducted all our experiments using PyTorch
framework [32], which allowed us to effectively deploy net-
works training, involving parallel computations, to GPUs. In
all cases we have trained our networks using crops of size
128×128 pixels. We have used random crops as well as
random multiple of 90° rotations and random horizontal and
vertical flips to augment our training data. We minimised all
objective functions using Adam optimizer [33] with learning
rate of 1e−3, and a warm up scheduling was enabled for thefirst epoch. For training synthesis neural network, we have
used an adversarial learning adopted from pix2pix [23]. Since
the network chosen to perform synthesis is relatively small, we
have used an adaptive gradient steps frequency [34] for both
generator and discriminator in order to avoid discriminator’s
overfitting and other instabilities.

E. Porting to Embedded System
We have used NVIDIA Jetson Xavier NX as a target
device, since it was proven to be an efficient low-cost so-
lution for various vision tasks in edge computing [8]. Inorder to accelerate the inference speed of our network and
make simulations of the proposed approach running on an
embedded system, we have applied the well-known techniques
of network quantization and layers fusion (pruning). Due to
specific choice of segmentation and synthesis networks in our
less computationally intensive regime to be the standard U-
Net architectures, the port of our models to the edge device
is relatively straightforward. Due to this reason we have used
NVIDIA TensorRT inference optimizer and runtime, which
performs all the optimizations, as well as other architecture-
specific tunings.
F . Evaluation Metrics
In order to evaluate our models and compare it to the
baselines, we have used two most common approaches for
the classification and semantic segmentation (i.e. classification
of each image pixel), namely mean intersection over union
(mIoU) and the Area Under the Curve (AUC) of a Receiver
Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve, given by the true pos-
itive and false positive rates. For more informative evaluation
both metrics are computed in a multi-class regime when young
and old crops are treated as different classes and in a binary
regime when both types of crops are treated as a single class.
As a reference, we have observed, that typical values of 60 %
of mIoU reported in various multi-class semantic segmentation
benchmarks [36] is considered as an acceptable segmentation
performance.
To assess the performance of our approach ported to em-
bedded system, we measure the inference speed per 12MPx
frame ( 3000×4000 px) in Frames Per Second (FPS), as well
as the power consumption of the hardware in watts (W).
IV. R ESULTS
At the first stage of our study we validate the designed
labels transfer. For this purpose we have randomly selected
10 frames out of the sequence used to construct the training
dataset. The proposed strategy was used to sample images
crops with corresponding labels transferred from orthophoto
data. For the same set of frames we have performed manual
labeling, which allowed to get the true labels for the sampled
crops and use them to perform a quantitative comparison. The
evaluation performed by the means of mIoU showed 95%
and90% for old and young crops respectively with the main
source of error attributed to the inconsistencies introduced by
independent manual labeling of frames and orthophoto. Such
findings demonstrate that the difference between the true labels
and the labels transferred from orthophoto with our proposed

V. C ONCLUSIONS
In this article, we have proposed and evaluated an idea
of incorporating knowledge about multispectral bands in the
neural network performing semantic segmentation of Hogweed
crops. We have collected and labeled two datasets consisting
of old and young hogweed crops from two different fields
within the same region, one of which involved only RGB
frames stream and is used for testing purposes, and another
one was collected with additional RGN camera on board. We
have proposed an idea of labeling frames by labeling the corre-
sponding orthophotos and we have successfully applied it for
both datasets. We also proposed an alignment strategy, which
allowed us to align RGB, RGN, and labels orthophotos be-
tween each other and RGB frames from the frame stream. With
this data, we have trained a U-Net and SegNet architectures to
perform semantic segmentation, and we have used a transfer
learning procedure to train a separate synthesis head consisting
of a another lighter network which performs the synthesis of
RGN data to facilitate further semantic segmentation task. We
have observed that this approach is at least 1.1 % better than
end-to-end training of either segmentation or both synthesis
and segmentation networks when only RGB data is involved.
We have also shown, that this training procedure allows to
obtain segmentation quality of the same level as when a real
RGN data is provided. We have ported our trained networks
to an embedded system and showed that they are capable
of performing processing part of a real mission targeting
precise location of hogweed crops. The proposed approach
can be scaled to other agricultural industry related application
including the diseases and insects detection.
